Newton vs Einstein in the solar system: a simulator

A Python solar-system simulator that integrates the planets with Newtonian gravity and then with General Relativity, and measures the difference — Mercury's famous 43 arcseconds per century come out of the simulation itself, together with the planets' 530", light bending, the Shapiro delay, and the small effects that compete with GR

In 1859 Urbain Le Verrier, fresh from predicting Neptune out of Uranus’ misbehaviour, reported that Mercury’s perihelion was drifting about 38 arcseconds per century faster than the known planets could explain (Newcomb later refined this to 43”). A planet inside Mercury’s orbit, Vulcan, was proposed, hunted for, and even “seen” a few times, but it never existed. In November 1915 Einstein computed the perihelion shift implied by his brand-new field equations and found 43” per century. He wrote that for a few days he was “beside myself with joyous excitement”.

That number is one of the great tests of General Relativity, and it is small enough to be delicate and large enough to be reproducible on a laptop. So I built gravsim: a Python package, nine lesson notebooks, a Streamlit lab and a 3-D web viewer that integrate the solar system first with Newton, then with Einstein, and make the difference measurable. This post walks through the physics and shows what comes out.

The 3-D viewer (Three.js in the browser, physics in Python) 70 years into a century-long run. Both the Newtonian and the relativistic orbits are drawn; the GR − Newton displacement is amplified about 2000× so that the arc of Mercury's perihelion markers, otherwise invisible, shows the relativistic advance running ahead of the Newtonian one.

Newton first: the clockwork solar system

The Newtonian problem is nine point masses pulling on each other,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.newton} \ddot{\mathbf{r}}i = -\sum{j\neq i} \frac{G m_j\,(\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j)}{|\mathbf{r}_i-\mathbf{r}_j|^3}, \end{equation}

started from the real state of the Sun and the eight planets at J2000.0, taken from the JPL Horizons ephemeris. Everything is done in astronomical units, days and \(GM\) values (DE440), in the barycentric J2000 ecliptic frame, so the Sun moves too: Jupiter drags it around the barycentre by about 1.6 solar radii.

A century of Newtonian motion for the Sun and eight planets (left) and the inner planets (right), integrated with an 8th-order Dormand–Prince scheme at relative tolerance $$10^{-12}$$.

Before claiming anything about relativity one has to be sure the numerics are not inventing a drift. The relativistic signal we are after is \(5\times10^{-7}\) radians per orbit of Mercury, a tenth of an arcsecond, and a fixed-step integrator produces a spurious perihelion precession of order \((2\pi/N)^2\) per orbit for \(N\) steps per orbit. With half-day steps, plain leapfrog “discovers” a precession of \(-2\times10^{5}\) arcseconds per century, four thousand times the effect we want to measure. The package therefore implements the usual ladder of integrators (Euler, symplectic Euler, leapfrog, RK4, Yoshida 4th and 6th order) as teaching material, and uses the adaptive DOP853 scheme for production runs, where the numerical precession of a two-body Kepler orbit over a century comes out at 0.001” per century. That is the noise floor.

Relative energy error over time. Symplectic schemes keep it bounded; Runge–Kutta drifts; the adaptive 8th-order scheme stays at the $$10^{-13}$$ level for a century.

Measuring a drift of 0.1 arcsecond per orbit

The instrument is the Laplace–Runge–Lenz (eccentricity) vector, which points at perihelion and is conserved exactly in the Kepler problem:

\begin{equation}\label{eq.lrl} \mathbf{e} = \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{h}}{GM} - \frac{\mathbf{r}}{r}, \qquad \mathbf{h}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}. \end{equation}

Its direction, expressed as the longitude of perihelion \(\varpi\), is computed at every sample of the run; a least-squares slope of \(\varpi(t)\) gives the precession rate. In the full N-body run \(\varpi\) wobbles by tens of arcseconds under the periodic pull of Venus and Jupiter, and the secular rate from the planets alone is Le Verrier’s 530” per century. The relativistic 43” sits on top of that, which is why the cleanest measurement is a difference: run the same initial state with and without the relativistic term, on the same time grid, and subtract the two \(\varpi(t)\) series. The planetary wobble and the integrator error cancel almost perfectly and the GR advance is left as a straight line.

Left: Mercury's osculating longitude of perihelion over a century in a Sun + 8 planet run, with and without the relativistic correction; the planets dominate. Right: the difference of the two runs. The slope is 42.98 ± 0.001 arcseconds per century.

From Einstein’s equations to the 43 arcseconds

Before the force law that the code integrates, here is where that law comes from, because the whole point of the project is that the number is derived, not fitted. The chain is: field equations → the metric outside the Sun → the geodesic equation → an orbit equation that is Newton’s plus one term → the precession.

The field equations. General Relativity replaces the gravitational potential by the metric \(g_{\mu\nu}\) of spacetime and Poisson’s equation by Einstein’s field equations,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.efe} R_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac{1}{2} R\, g_{\mu\nu} = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}\, T_{\mu\nu}, \end{equation}

where the Ricci tensor \(R_{\mu\nu}\) and the scalar \(R = g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu}\) are built from second derivatives of the metric, and \(T_{\mu\nu}\) is the energy–momentum of matter. Outside the Sun \(T_{\mu\nu} = 0\), and taking the trace of \eqref{eq.efe} gives \(R = 0\), so the vacuum equations are simply \(R_{\mu\nu} = 0\): ten coupled nonlinear partial differential equations for the ten components of \(g_{\mu\nu}\). Matter still enters, through the boundary condition that far from the Sun the field must reduce to Newton’s: comparing the geodesic equation below with Newton’s second law in the weak-field, slow-motion limit forces \(g_{00} \to -(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)\) with \(\Phi = -GM/r\). That identification is also what fixes the constant \(8\pi G/c^4\) in front of \(T_{\mu\nu}\).

The Schwarzschild solution. For a static, spherically symmetric source the vacuum equations have a unique solution, found by Karl Schwarzschild within weeks of Einstein’s paper (and, by Birkhoff’s theorem, the only one even if the star pulsates radially):

\begin{equation}\label{eq.schw} ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)c^2\,dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right)^{-1} dr^2 + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\phi^2\right), \qquad r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}. \end{equation}

For the Sun \(r_s = 2.95\) km, and at Mercury’s orbit \(r_s/r \approx 5\times10^{-8}\). That ratio is the size of everything relativistic in the inner solar system, and it is why the effect took until 1859 to notice and until 1915 to explain.

Geodesics. A planet is a test body, so it follows a geodesic of \eqref{eq.schw}: it extremises the proper time \(\tau\) along its path. The equations of motion come from the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2} g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^\mu \dot{x}^\nu\) with \(\dot{\ } = d/d\tau\). Two coordinates are cyclic, so two quantities are conserved along the orbit, and spherical symmetry lets us put the orbit in the plane \(\theta = \pi/2\):

\begin{equation}\label{eq.geo} E = \left(1-\frac{r_s}{r}\right) c^2\, \dot t, \qquad L = r^2 \dot\phi . \end{equation}

\(E\) is the energy per unit mass including rest energy, \(L\) the angular momentum per unit mass: Kepler’s second law survives intact, only with proper time as the clock. The third equation is the normalisation \(g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu = -c^2\) of the four-velocity, which with \eqref{eq.geo} becomes a radial energy equation,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.veff} \tfrac{1}{2}\dot r^2 + V_{\rm eff}(r) = \frac{E^2 - c^4}{2c^2}, \qquad V_{\rm eff}(r) = -\frac{GM}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} - \frac{GM L^2}{c^2 r^3}. \end{equation}

This is the whole of planetary motion in one line. The first two terms of \(V_{\rm eff}\) are exactly Newton’s effective potential, gravity plus the centrifugal barrier; the third is the relativistic correction. It is attractive, it falls off as \(1/r^3\), and near a compact mass it overwhelms the centrifugal barrier, which is where the innermost stable circular orbit and the photon sphere come from (more on that below). Near the Sun it is a whisper: at Mercury’s orbit it is \(10^{-8}\) of the Newtonian term. The simulation integrates \eqref{eq.veff} as it stands in the “exact Schwarzschild” mode.

The orbit equation. Follow Binet: use \(u = 1/r\) as the variable and \(\phi\) as the parameter. From \eqref{eq.geo}, \(\dot r = -L\, du/d\phi\), so \eqref{eq.veff} becomes a first-order equation in \(u(\phi)\); differentiating it once with respect to \(\phi\) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq.binet} \frac{d^2 u}{d\phi^2} + u = \frac{GM}{L^2} + \frac{3GM}{c^2}\, u^2 . \end{equation}

Without the last term this is Newton’s orbit equation, whose solution is the conic \(u_0(\phi) = \frac{GM}{L^2}\left(1 + e\cos\phi\right)\): a closed ellipse with the perihelion fixed at \(\phi = 0\) forever. The last term is all that General Relativity adds to the shape of a planetary orbit, and it is small: writing it in terms of the Newtonian solution,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.epsilon} \frac{3GM}{c^2}u_0^2 = \epsilon\,\frac{GM}{L^2}\left(1 + e\cos\phi\right)^2, \qquad \epsilon \equiv \frac{3\,(GM)^2}{c^2 L^2} = \frac{3GM}{c^2 a(1-e^2)} \approx 8\times10^{-8} \end{equation}

for Mercury, where \(L^2 = GM a(1-e^2)\) relates the angular momentum to the osculating ellipse.

Perturbation theory. Write \(u = u_0 + u_1\) with \(u_1 = O(\epsilon)\) and keep first order. The correction obeys a driven harmonic oscillator,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.pert} \frac{d^2 u_1}{d\phi^2} + u_1 = \epsilon\,\frac{GM}{L^2}\left(1 + 2e\cos\phi + e^2\cos^2\phi\right). \end{equation}

The constant and the \(\cos^2\phi = \tfrac12(1+\cos 2\phi)\) pieces on the right drive bounded, periodic responses: they distort the ellipse by one part in \(10^{7}\) and nothing accumulates. The \(2e\cos\phi\) piece is different: it drives the oscillator at its own frequency, which is resonance, and the response grows without bound,

\begin{equation}\label{eq.secular} u_1^{\rm (sec)} = \epsilon\,\frac{GM}{L^2}\; e\,\phi \sin\phi . \end{equation}

Add it to \(u_0\) and use \(\cos\phi + \epsilon\phi\sin\phi \approx \cos\big[(1-\epsilon)\phi\big]\), valid as long as \(\epsilon\phi \ll 1\), that is for many thousands of orbits:

\begin{equation}\label{eq.orbit} u(\phi) \approx \frac{GM}{L^2}\Big{1 + e\cos\big[(1-\epsilon)\phi\big]\Big}. \end{equation}

The orbit is still an ellipse to this accuracy, but the angle has been rescaled: \(r\) returns to its minimum not after \(\phi = 2\pi\) but after \((1-\epsilon)\phi = 2\pi\), so each perihelion arrives \(2\pi/(1-\epsilon) - 2\pi \approx 2\pi\epsilon\) later in angle than the last. The ellipse turns, in the direction of motion, by

\begin{equation}\label{eq.advance} \Delta\phi = 2\pi\epsilon = \frac{6\pi\,(GM)^2}{c^2 L^2} = \frac{6\pi G M}{c^2\, a\,(1-e^2)} \end{equation}

per revolution. This is the formula Einstein wrote down on 18 November 1915. It depends on the orbit only through \(a(1-e^2)\), the semi-latus rectum, which is why Mercury, the closest and most eccentric planet, is the one that shows it.

The number. For the Sun \(GM/c^2 = 1.4766\) km; for Mercury \(a = 5.791\times10^{7}\) km and \(e = 0.2056\), so \(a(1-e^2) = 5.546\times10^{7}\) km and

\begin{equation}\label{eq.number} \Delta\phi = 6\pi \times \frac{1.4766}{5.546\times10^{7}} = 5.02\times10^{-7}\ {\rm rad} = 0.1035’’ \end{equation}

per orbit. Mercury completes \(36525/87.969 = 415.2\) orbits per Julian century, which gives \(42.98''\) per century, Le Verrier’s missing piece to two decimal places. The perturbative treatment is accurate to relative order \(\epsilon\) itself; the exact Schwarzschild advance, which gravsim computes by integrating \eqref{eq.binet} numerically, differs from \eqref{eq.advance} by \(10^{-4}\) arcseconds per century, as the lesson on black-hole orbits checks.

Where the force law comes from. The N-body code cannot integrate a metric per body; it needs an acceleration. Expanding the geodesic equation of a nearly-Newtonian metric in powers of \(1/c^2\) (the post-Newtonian expansion) gives the Newtonian acceleration plus a correction of relative size \(v^2/c^2\); the result in harmonic coordinates is equation \eqref{eq.pn1} below. It contains the same physics as \eqref{eq.binet}, and indeed averaging it over a Kepler ellipse reproduces \eqref{eq.advance} exactly. For several massive bodies the same expansion applied to the full field equations, keeping every body as a source, gives the Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann equations that the ephemerides use.

Einstein in the N-body code: the first post-Newtonian correction

In the weak-field, slow-motion limit the geodesic equation reduces to Newton’s second law with the Newtonian acceleration plus a correction of relative size \(v^2/c^2 \sim GM/(c^2 r) \sim 10^{-8}\) for Mercury. In harmonic coordinates, and with the parametrised post-Newtonian (PPN) parameters \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) left explicit (they measure, respectively, the nonlinearity of gravity and the spatial curvature per unit mass; General Relativity has \(\beta = \gamma = 1\)),

\begin{equation}\label{eq.pn1} \mathbf{a}_{1\mathrm{PN}} = \frac{GM}{c^2 r^3}\left[\left(2(\beta+\gamma)\frac{GM}{r} - \gamma v^2\right)\mathbf{r} + 2(1+\gamma)(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{v}\right], \end{equation}

which for GR (\(\beta=\gamma=1\)) is the familiar \((4GM/r - v^2)\,\mathbf{r} + 4(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{v}\). Averaged over an orbit (Gauss’s perturbation equations do the bookkeeping) this force turns the ellipse by the PPN generalisation of \eqref{eq.advance},

\begin{equation}\label{eq.precession} \Delta\varpi = \frac{2+2\gamma-\beta}{3}\,\frac{6\pi GM}{c^2 a(1-e^2)} \end{equation}

per revolution. For Mercury the GR value is the 42.98” per century of \eqref{eq.number}; the simulation, which knows nothing of the derivation above and only integrates \eqref{eq.pn1}, measures 42.98 ± 0.001. The same force applied to all planets gives 8.64” for Venus, 3.85” for the Earth and 1.35” for Mars (predicted 8.62, 3.84, 1.35), and plotting the measured advance per orbit against \(a(1-e^2)\) puts every planet on the \(1/(a(1-e^2))\) line of equation \eqref{eq.precession}.

Relativistic advance per orbit measured for each planet in the century-long N-body runs (GR minus Newton), against the analytic $$6\pi GM_\odot/(c^2 a(1-e^2))$$.

JPL does not use the test-particle formula: its ephemerides integrate the Einstein–Infeld–Hoffmann equations, in which every body curves spacetime and the corrections involve all the mutual potentials and velocities. gravsim implements those too (with \(\beta\), \(\gamma\)). For Mercury the EIH and the Sun-only correction differ by \(10^{-5}\) arcseconds per century, and an independent oracle, the REBOUNDx implementation of the same equations, agrees with gravsim’s positions to 35 metres after ten years.

Le Verrier’s anomaly, from the data

The ephemeris can also play the role of the observations. Horizons includes relativity; a Newtonian run started from the same state drifts away from it, and the drift is exactly the missing term.

Mercury's distance from the JPL Horizons ephemeris over a century. The Newtonian run ends up 5700 km away; with the relativistic term the residual is 180 km, most of it the unmodelled Moon and asteroids. Fitting Mercury's perihelion in the Horizons series itself and in the Newtonian run gives a difference of 43.0" per century: Le Verrier's anomaly, recovered from modern data.

Seeing it: turn down the speed of light

At the true value of \(c\) nothing is visible to the eye, so the lessons and the viewer have an exaggeration knob. Scaling \(c\) down by 100 multiplies the 1PN precession by \(10^4\) while keeping \(v/c \approx 0.02\), so the expansion is still valid; Mercury’s perihelion then advances about 0.3° per orbit and the ellipse visibly turns into a rosette. The measured rate stays within 0.2% of equation \eqref{eq.precession} evaluated with the reduced \(c\).

Left: every 20th of 200 orbits of Mercury with $$c/100$$; the ellipse turns by 57°. Right: the longitude of perihelion over 50 orbits and the analytic slope.
The same scenario in the viewer, with the perihelion markers sweeping around the Sun.

What if gravity were a different theory?

Because \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are explicit in the force laws, one can ask the simulator what a different metric theory would do. Running the two-body problem with \((\beta,\gamma)\) equal to \((1,1)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,0)\) and \((2,1)\) gives precession rates in the ratios \(1 : 4/3 : 1/3 : 2/3\), as the factor \((2+2\gamma-\beta)/3\) in equation \eqref{eq.precession} requires, to four parts in \(10^4\). Mercury alone constrains only this combination; the degeneracy is broken by light, for which the deflection depends on \((1+\gamma)/2\) only, and it is the Cassini measurement \(\gamma - 1 = (2.1\pm2.3)\times10^{-5}\) that pins things down.

Mercury's perihelion advance relative to Newton for four choices of the PPN parameters, each against the prediction $$(2+2\gamma-\beta)/3 \times 42.98$$" per century.

Beyond the expansion: exact Schwarzschild geodesics

The 1PN term is the first order of an expansion in \(GM/(c^2 r)\), superb around the Sun (\(10^{-8}\)) and useless near a black hole. gravsim also integrates the exact timelike and null geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric, that is, equations \eqref{eq.geo} and \eqref{eq.veff} without any expansion. Everything follows from the effective potential of \eqref{eq.veff}, whose \(-GML^2/(c^2 r^3)\) term, a whisper at Mercury, becomes dominant at small \(r\): it creates the innermost stable circular orbit at \(6GM/c^2\), the photon sphere at \(3GM/c^2\), and orbits that whirl many times near periapsis before zooming back out. For Mercury the exact geodesic advance agrees with the 1PN formula to \(10^{-4}\) arcseconds per century, which is the measure of how good the expansion is.

An exact Schwarzschild geodesic launched at $$r = 20\,GM/c^2$$ with periapsis at $$4.6\,GM/c^2$$, just outside the separatrix: the body whirls 2.8 times around the hole per radial period, an advance of 650° per orbit. The rings mark the horizon, the photon sphere and the ISCO.

Light: Eddington 1919 and the Shapiro delay

Photons follow null geodesics, \(u'' + u = 3GM u^2/c^2\) with \(u = 1/r\), and a ray with impact parameter \(b\) is bent by

\begin{equation}\label{eq.deflection} \delta = \frac{1+\gamma}{2}\,\frac{4GM}{c^2 b}, \end{equation}

twice Soldner’s Newtonian corpuscle value of 1801 because space itself is curved (\(\gamma = 1\)). At the solar limb the exact geodesic gives 1.7512”, the number Eddington’s 1919 eclipse expedition set out to measure. The same geometry delays a radar signal grazing the Sun: integrating the coordinate time along the null geodesic from the Earth to Mars at superior conjunction gives a one-way Shapiro delay of 123.6 μs, within \(10^{-4}\) of the weak-field formula \((1+\gamma)\,(GM/c^3)\ln\!\big[(r_e+r_r+R)/(r_e+r_r-R)\big]\).

Left: rays past the Sun with their deviation from straight exaggerated $$10^4$$ times. Right: deflection against impact parameter from the exact null geodesic, with Einstein's and Soldner's lines and the 1919 and modern VLBI measurements scaled to the limb.

What else competes with GR?

Forty-three arcseconds is a difference between a measurement of 574” and a Newtonian prediction of 531”, so one must know every other contribution at the 0.1” level. gravsim models the next three and puts them on one chart.

Everything that moves Mercury's perihelion, measured in the simulation. The dotted line is the precision of a century of ranging.
  • The Sun’s oblateness. A quadrupole \(J_2 = 2.2\times10^{-7}\) adds a \(1/r^4\) force whose secular effect, by Kaula’s formulas, is \(\tfrac32 n J_2 (R_\odot/p)^2 = 0.028\)” per century for Mercury; the run gives 0.0278. It has the same \(a\)-dependence structure as a change in \(\beta\), which is why \(J_2\) and \(\beta\) were degenerate until Venus and MESSENGER ranging separated them.
  • Frame dragging. The Sun’s spin (\(J_\odot = 1.9\times10^{41}\) kg m² s⁻¹) produces the Lense–Thirring acceleration \(\mathbf{a} = \frac{2G}{c^2 r^3}\big[\tfrac{3}{r^2}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{J})\,\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{J}\big]\), which drags the node forward and the perihelion backward: \(-0.002\)” per century for Mercury, measured as \(-0.0020\). Amplifying \(J\) by \(10^5\) reproduces the analytic node and perigee rates to four decimal places.
  • The Moon. Replacing the Earth–Moon barycentre by the Earth and the Moon changes Mercury’s Newtonian rate by \(-0.0025\)” per century and leaves the relativistic difference untouched to \(10^{-7}\). The Moon itself is a nice bonus: its perigee advances 40.6° per year, the largest “precession” in the solar system and purely Newtonian, driven by the Sun’s tide, and its node regresses at 19.4°/yr, both matching the ephemeris over the same span to 0.01°/yr.
Left: two years of the Moon's orbit about the Earth with its perigees marked. Right: the longitudes of perigee and node over ten years with their secular fits.

Running it

Everything is in the gravsim repository: the package with its own force laws, integrators and measurement toolkit (JPL Horizons and REBOUND are used only as independent references), nine lesson notebooks that put the physics before the code, a seven-lab Streamlit app, the 3-D viewer, theory pages with the derivations, and a validation script with 64 checks that reproduces every number quoted here.

git clone https://github.com/ftahas/gravsim && cd gravsim
scripts/setup_env.sh
.venv/bin/python scripts/validate.py      # the 64 checks, about two minutes
.venv/bin/gravsim-web --port 8000         # the 3-D viewer at http://localhost:8000
The viewer's Earth-following camera, with the Moon's orbit around the moving Earth.

It was built as a guided undergraduate project, and the thing I like most about it is that the headline result is not a number copied from a textbook: integrate Newton, integrate Einstein, subtract, and 42.98” per century is what you get.
